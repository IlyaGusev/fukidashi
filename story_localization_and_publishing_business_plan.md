# More stories. In your language.
*A reader-first publishing and localization business for novels, comics, and manga*

**Discover the stories you have been missing. Read complete, authorized editions. Keep the voice of the original.**

> Your next favorite story may already exist—just not in a language you can comfortably read. We find compelling books, comics, and manga, work with their rights holders, and bring carefully reviewed editions to a new audience. Readers discover and buy the stories. Creators participate in their success. A whole-work localization platform makes each new edition a more repeatable production.

**Business in one sentence:** A specialist digital publisher that licenses stories with unmet language demand, produces complete, professionally reviewed editions, and sells them directly to readers, with an optional production workspace for publishing partners.

**Plan date:** 23 September 2026.  
**Research basis:** Sources and research notes dated 22 September 2026.  
**Working establishment:** Netherlands-based publisher and consumer seller.  
**Initial market hypothesis:** German-language adult fantasy and related series fiction, marketed first to readers in Germany.  
**Recommended launch:** One source–target language operation, one production format, and one released edition before broadening the catalog. English-to-German prose is the starting hypothesis; illustrated stories are the first format expansion.  
**Commercial status:** Proposed business, product, terms, budgets, and performance targets—not an operating service, acquired catalog, or validated forecast.

## 1. Vision, positioning, and business strategy

### 1.1 Sell the story, not the translation task

The customer-facing product is a story worth reading. A reader should not need to upload source material, understand model context windows, approve glossaries, or finance a private translation of an entire book. They should discover a compelling title, read a substantial sample, understand the edition they are buying, and receive a finished work.

The promise is:

> **More stories worth reading, made available in your language—with permission, care, and the character of the original intact.**

For a novel, that means a consistent narrative voice, preserved ambiguity, reliable terminology, and dialogue that still sounds like the characters. For a comic or manga volume, it also means that the joke still works when its payoff arrives, the correct character speaks, the reading order makes sense, and the words belong inside the page rather than merely inside a spreadsheet.

One accepted edition can serve many readers during its licensed term. The company invests in the work once, maintains the published version, and earns subsequent sales without regenerating the translation for every purchase. The relevant production unit is **work × target language × approved edition**.

### 1.2 One company, a shared production foundation

The company combines four responsibilities: selecting stories, acquiring sufficient rights, producing dependable editions, and reaching readers. Its platform supports those responsibilities; it is not a substitute for them.

The common production foundation handles immutable sources, whole-work and series context, translation units, evidence, editorial decisions, approvals, costs, and release packages. Format-specific modules handle manuscript structure or visual pages. A shared commercial layer handles licenses, offers, orders, entitlements, corrections, and royalties.

The same production capabilities may later be sold to professional publishers and agencies. Those customers buy a workflow and retain their publishing relationships. The company's own catalog requires independent publishing licenses. A professional customer's confidential manuscript, approved translation, artwork, or series glossary never becomes retail inventory merely because it passed through the same software.

### 1.3 Reader-first, with a disciplined secondary offer

Reader sales are the principal commercial test. Authors and creators are primarily rights and supply partners, not people who must first buy a translation package. Selected co-funding is possible, but its economics and contractual obligations must be explicit.

Professional production work can contribute revenue and expose the platform to demanding real workflows. It should remain capacity-capped and separately accounted for. A paid publisher pilot demonstrates demand for production assistance; it does not demonstrate that readers will buy the company's editions. Conversely, a successful novel does not prove a recurring software business.

The initial organization should not attempt to launch a general bookstore, a full-service localization agency, and a mature software platform simultaneously. Build the controlled production core for the first catalog, then commercialize professional access where the existing workflow and available capacity genuinely support it.

### 1.4 A broad destination, a narrow first release

The long-term catalog can span novels, comics, and manga under a recognizable editorial identity. That does not require the first release to support every format or source language.

The recommended starting hypothesis is **English-to-German adult fantasy and related series fiction**, with one prose edition released first. Seek up to three related title-language opportunities from at least two rights holders, reserve production funding for two editions, and authorize further work in stages.

The first illustrated edition should serve the same reader segment and, where practical, the same language operation—for example, an English-language creator-owned fantasy comic whose relevant rights are available. Japanese-to-German page-based manga remains a credible later production lane, but it requires its own bilingual team, rights relationships, extraction evaluation, and commercial validation. It is not treated as a free addition to English-to-German capability.

This sequence is a proposed prioritization, not a claim that prose has been proven more attractive than comics. Change the first format before committing development and production money if a materially stronger rights-and-reader opportunity emerges. Once a cohort is selected, avoid changing language, format, and audience at the same time.

### 1.5 The repeatable business cycle

```text
Relevant reader demand and a compelling title
    → Available rights and permitted sample
    → Scoped publishing agreement and production investment
    → Whole-work localization and human editorial approval
    → One stable, complete digital edition
    → Direct reader purchases and reliable delivery
    → A relevant next title or series volume
    → Better-informed licensing and production decisions
```

The potential advantage is cumulative: a useful catalog, trusted editions, approved series knowledge, rights-holder relationships, and a reader audience that returns. None arises merely from access to an inexpensive model. Each must improve the next production or purchasing decision.

## 2. Readers, rights partners, and market selection

### 2.1 The first reader

Recruit people who already buy digital fiction in the target language and can describe a real reading gap. Someone who names a missing series, author, or subgenre is a better initial prospect than someone who is generally enthusiastic about AI.

| Reader situation | Job to be done | Offer to test |
|---|---|---|
| A desired story lacks a suitable target-language edition | Read that particular work | Complete licensed edition with a persuasive sample |
| A translated first volume created interest in the series | Continue without an unexplained gap | A credible next-release plan with separately secured rights |
| A genre reader wants more of a familiar experience | Discover another story that fits their tastes | A tightly curated catalog across relevant storytelling formats |
| A reader has already bought and enjoyed an edition | Find the next worthwhile purchase | A genuinely available related title and optional release notifications |
| A visual-story reader dislikes awkward or incomplete localization | Read the story without fighting the page | Clear dialogue, correct reading order, thoughtful lettering, and complete volumes |

Do not assume that all prose readers also buy manga, or that visual-story fans will buy novels because both are fantasy. Test cross-format interest. The shared editorial identity should help readers choose; it should not force unrelated inventory into a single feed.

### 2.2 Rights and production partners

The first rights partner should control a suitable work and the relevant target-language rights. A strong candidate has an established source-language publishing record, a clear rights chain, usable source assets, and willingness to authorize the actual AI-assisted workflow.

A large English- or Japanese-language following is not automatically a German acquisition channel. Qualify target-language reach through relevant subscriber segments, previous requests, communities, or permissioned introductions. Follower counts alone do not establish a reachable buying audience.

Editors, literary translators, and letterers are paid production and evaluation partners. Association membership or a public business listing is not evidence that they endorse AI-assisted localization. Recruit willing, qualified professionals around a concrete review scope, actual rates, credits, and responsibilities.

A professional-software prospect has a different qualification profile: recurring production, an involved editor and letterer where relevant, a near-term eligible title, a budget owner, and a specific handoff the platform can improve.

### 2.3 Market evidence and its boundaries

The research notes report the following market context:

| Evidence | Relevance and limitation |
|---|---|
| Germany recorded 37.2 million paid ebook purchases, a €7.01 average paid price, and 3.4 million buyers in the cited 2025 measurement; fiction represented 88.2% of ebook revenue. [R01] | Establishes an existing paid fiction market, not demand for a new independent store or a particular translated title. A €9.99 edition must justify its price. |
| German paid ebook units declined 4.5% in 2025 while revenue was approximately flat. [R01] | The thesis is better title selection and availability, not an automatic market boom. |
| German book-trade revenue was approximately €9.62 billion in 2025, down 2.7%. [R02] | Broad category context, not an obtainable-market calculation. |
| KVB Boekwerk reported approximately €697 million in Dutch book-market revenue for 2025. [R03] | Supports investigating the Netherlands later; does not identify the size of this translation opportunity. |
| J-Novel Club documents translated catalogs, previews, individual digital purchases, memberships, and series engagement. [R04] [R48] | A recognizable commercial mechanism across prose and manga; no proof of this startup's margins or target-language demand. |

The German ebook report covers a defined consumer trade market, excludes subscriptions and free downloads, and uses a YouGov panel. Its population basis changed for 2025, with 2024 restated for comparison. These numbers should not be presented as all digital reading, all visual storytelling, or a forecast for the proposed genre. [R01]

### 2.4 Choose titles where four conditions overlap

A production candidate needs a plausible answer to four questions: can the rights be obtained, can the promised edition be produced, can relevant readers be reached, and can contribution recover the investment within the license term?

Use a title scorecard, but keep editorial judgment visible. An acclaimed book with expensive rights and no reachable target-language readership may be a worse first investment than a smaller title with excellent audience fit and a cooperative rights holder.

Check current target-language availability through authorized publisher and retailer information before presenting a gap as real. No named title in this plan is represented as untranslated, unlicensed, or available to acquire.

### 2.5 Initial validation cohort

A proposed first program comprises 30 target-language reader interviews, 10–15 rights-holder conversations, and paid sample assessment by at least two suitable bilingual reviewers. When the visual module is activated, involve a letterer before quoting or promising page-ready output.

Ask readers about their last unavailable book or comic, the alternative they chose, recent purchases, discovery routes, preferred devices, and reasons for abandoning checkout. Test a real, permitted sample rather than asking whether they would hypothetically pay for AI translation.

For the first released edition, aim for 10 genuine purchases to test operations, then approximately 50–100 retained purchases to understand an initial commercial response. These are learning gates, not title break-even. In the prose base case, recovering a €3,000 edition requires 861 retained purchases after the modeled acquisition allowance and before overhead.

At illustrative purchase conversion rates of 3% and 5%, 861 retained purchases would require approximately 28,700 and 17,220 qualified visits respectively, assuming the conversion measure itself counts retained purchases. These are arithmetic examples, not benchmarks. They make the acquisition requirement concrete.

### 2.6 Bottom-up opportunity sizing

Model released title-language editions, sales by edition and channel, ex-VAT prices, royalties, transactions, support, marketing, and production. Bound releases by signed rights, editorial capacity, and capital. Bound backlist sales by the remaining license term.

Count paid professional production once: either the direct publisher account or an agency's aggregated usage, not both for the same work. Keep reader revenue and service revenue distinct. A list of all books the engine could theoretically process is neither a licensed catalog nor a market estimate.

## 3. Catalog development and publishing partnerships

### 3.1 Build an editorial identity, not an inventory dump

The launch catalog should make sense to one reader. A practical configuration is the first two books of a series and a compatible standalone from another rights holder. An illustrated title can extend that identity once the production and reader-format gates are met.

Prefer complete books, complete one-shots, or volumes with a clear continuation plan. Do not advertise an entire translated series when only its first installment is contracted. Secure realistic sequel access before making continuity the main acquisition promise.

The small catalog should feel curated: a distinctive premise, substantial samples, accurate series order, dependable editions, and a visible next release. It should not feel like a collection of whatever material happened to be easiest to translate.

### 3.2 Title-acquisition record

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Work | Title, creator or author, source language, source edition, genre, series position, complete/ongoing status |
| Rights | Controlling party, chain of authority, existing language grants, options, territory, formats, channels, term |
| Audience | Specific target-language demand, permitted audience access, relevant previous requests, comparable reader response |
| Assets | Manuscript or page files, clean artwork where available, source scripts, cover, metadata, fonts and promotional material |
| Production | Length, complexity, reviewer availability, lettering needs, accessibility work, sample findings, quoted budget |
| Commercial | Proposed price, royalty basis, advance or guarantee, acquisition route, expected recovery window |
| Continuation | Sequel availability, option boundaries, approval process, next useful title |
| Status | Research lead, contact made, evaluation permitted, negotiation, signed, production-approved, released, expired |

A publicly visible work, official store, or creator profile is a discovery route—not evidence that translation and digital rights are available. Creator-owned material still requires review of existing grants, collaborators, and third-party content.

### 3.3 The partner proposition

> **Your story already exists. Let’s give it another audience.**
>
> We license an agreed digital language edition, organize the translation and editorial production, and sell it to readers. You retain ownership of the original and rights outside the grant. We agree the workflow, approvals, territories, channels, reporting, and revenue share before production. The first commitment is one title and an approved sample, not your whole catalog.

The company-funded pilot should not require the creator to buy production services. Co-funding may be appropriate when both parties prefer it, but it changes the investment and royalty arrangement and must be modeled separately.

### 3.4 Publishing terms to negotiate

The following is a commercial brief for counsel, not contract language or a statement of market-standard terms.

| Term | Proposed approach |
|---|---|
| Work and source | Name the exact work and source revision; identify excluded third-party material |
| Grant | Translation, necessary production copies, publication, making available, sale, delivery, and agreed promotional use |
| Language and territory | Specify both independently; one language does not mean worldwide rights |
| Formats and channels | Explicit own-store permission; separately address downloads, browser reading, other retailers, subscriptions, libraries, print, and audio |
| Term | Negotiate a bounded initial term; 24–36 months is an illustrative discussion range, not an assumed entitlement |
| Exclusivity | Limit any exclusive grant to the language, format, territory, and term needed to justify investment |
| Production process | Disclose AI use, approved vendors, subcontracting, review standard, and restrictions on training or unrelated reuse |
| Editorial control | Approve representative style and glossary choices; define final proof, turnaround, significant changes, and dispute handling |
| Money | Define royalty basis, VAT treatment, refunds, allowed deductions, reserves, statements, currency, and payment dates |
| Advances and guarantees | Identify recoupability, nonreturnability, recoupment pool, and any minimum guarantee separately |
| Attribution and contributions | Agree author/creator, translator, editor, letterer, and other applicable credits; obtain contributor permissions |
| Price authority | Identify responsibility for lawful publisher-set prices, coordinated changes, collections, and special offers |
| Publication and reversion | Set publication deadlines, non-exploitation and breach procedures, expiry, renewal, and existing-buyer treatment |
| Sequels | A bounded option or first discussion for named follow-on works, with a decision deadline—not an indefinite claim |
| Corrections | Separate routine defects from material rewrites; preserve version and approval records |
| Audit and disputes | Rights warranties, proportionate remedies, records, reporting inspection, and an agreed claims process |

The financial illustrations use two possible negotiation structures: 30% of ex-VAT retained sales for a prose edition, and 50% of defined net receipts for a visual edition. These are alternative deal assumptions, not prescribed rates by format. The signed contract determines the actual calculation. When the licensor is a publisher, describe payment as a rights-holder share unless the creator's pass-through has been verified.

### 3.5 Reader-informed commissioning

Maintain a demand queue, but distinguish interest from authorization. Readers can request a title and language without being promised publication. Public covers, excerpts, and translated samples require their own permission where applicable.

Use the states **requested → rights discussion → licensed → sample approved → commissioned → in production → approved → released**. Add a funded/preorder state only when the relevant rights, funding conditions, and consumer process are ready.

The demand queue becomes valuable when it changes a real acquisition decision. It is not a license pool. Avoid taking money on an unlicensed title and describing the transaction as merely a vote.

## 4. Reader experience and commercial offers

### 4.1 The complete purchase journey

A reader encounters a story through a creator, a genre community, a permitted campaign, or title discovery. The landing page opens a substantial sample without unnecessary account friction. It clearly identifies the language, format, length, creator, series position, availability, review process, price, and delivery conditions. After payment, the reader receives the exact approved edition and a clear support route.

The next interaction should be useful: an available related title, a relevant sequel announcement where marketing is permitted, or a correction to a purchased edition. The business does not need a social feed or an elaborate recommendation engine to make that experience work.

### 4.2 Title-page requirements

| Element | What the reader should learn |
|---|---|
| Cover and story hook | What kind of experience the title offers |
| Description | The premise and stakes, without invented awards or endorsements |
| Catalog context | Genre, author/creator, series order, complete/ongoing status |
| Sample | Narrative voice, dialogue, reading comfort, and visual treatment where relevant |
| Edition information | Target language, AI use, actual human review, and contributor credits |
| Format | EPUB or supported page-based format; tested delivery routes and device guidance |
| Price | Correct total consumer price for the offer and territory |
| Access | Downloads versus hosted access, account requirements, and applicable limitations |
| Support | Delivery help, defect reports, withdrawal where applicable, refunds, and corrections |
| Next title | A relevant available purchase, not an uncontracted release promise |

The page should sell without AI in its headline, but production disclosures should be visible before purchase. “Fully reviewed” is only appropriate when the promised review actually occurred.

### 4.3 Proposed offers

| Offer | Planning treatment | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Authorized sample | Free | Permission for the sample and promotional assets is required |
| Complete prose ebook | Investigate €7.99–€9.99; the German worked example uses €9.99 | One stable edition, not a bespoke translation request |
| Complete visual volume | The worked example uses €7.99 for a qualifying Dutch ebook transaction | Destination tax, licensed formats, and actual production scope determine the offer |
| Related-title collection | A €19.99 three-visual-volume example is modeled as an expansion option | Rights, lawful price structure, and title-level receipt allocation must permit it |
| Licensed reader-funded edition | Later, with a published price, threshold, and delivery terms | Not part of the first completed-edition checkout |
| Membership | Later, after catalog depth and repeat behavior justify it | No promise of unlimited translation of arbitrary requested works |

These are price hypotheses. They are not a recommendation to apply different national tax cases to the same sale or run unrestricted discounts in fixed-price markets. The first German visual edition would require its own approved price and tax configuration; the Dutch example is a separately scoped sensitivity, not its launch quote.

### 4.4 Delivery by format

**Prose:** deliver a stable EPUB with correct chapter navigation, emphasis, scene breaks, language metadata, cover rights, and tested reading instructions. A mandatory proprietary reader app is unnecessary.

**Comics and manga:** add a tested page-based reading experience with correct direction, page order, spreads, zoom, keyboard access, and resume behavior. Supply downloadable files only where the license and accessible-content strategy support them. The browser reader is a format-specific requirement, not a reason to delay the first prose sale.

Downloads and indefinite hosted access are different promises. Do not advertise “yours forever” when the agreement only supports revocable hosting. Negotiate and explain what existing purchasers retain when sales stop or the head license expires.

### 4.5 Retention begins with another good story

Offer a clear series sequence and a relevant next title. Measure second purchases only when another suitable edition was actually available during the observation window. A reader cannot prove sequel demand when there is no sequel to buy.

Track repeat contribution rather than email opens or retail revenue alone. Test whether illustrated and prose titles share an audience before relying on cross-format retention. Keep optional release notifications separate from purchase fulfillment and mandatory transactional messages.

### 4.6 Direct-first, not necessarily direct-only

Direct sales provide a way to learn about samples, discovery, checkout, delivery, support, and repeat orders. Other retailers can be complementary when contracts permit them.

Distribution terms matter independently of the underlying copyright license. KDP Select requires enrolled ebooks to remain digitally exclusive to the Kindle Store during enrollment, subject to its stated public-library exception. Check the exact translated edition and full terms before combining retailer participation with direct sales. [R09]

## 5. Competitive landscape and differentiation

### 5.1 Compete for a complete reading or production outcome

The alternatives include licensed publishers, integrated localization-and-distribution platforms, specialist translation products, open-source tools, and experienced human agencies. The reader may also choose the original, wait, or buy something else.

| Alternative | Evidence in the research record | Strategic implication |
|---|---|---|
| **J-Novel Club** | Describes translated prose and manga, previews, individual purchases, membership, and release/download policies. [R04] [R48] | Learn from catalog identity, samples, series cadence, and the distinction between prepublication material and finished editions. |
| **Amazon Kindle Translate** | The researched FAQ describes an invite-only beta, free during beta, and limits including direct editing of translated text. [R05] | Independent editorial control and distribution must offer a real advantage; model access alone is not differentiation. |
| **BookTranslator.ai / BookTranslate.ai** | Advertise book translation, contextual processing, whole-book analysis, and consistency-related workflows. [R06] [R07] | Whole-book context is not a unique headline. Compare delivered quality, authorization terms, review effort, and files. |
| **Mantra / Mantra Engine** | Describes manga translation, typesetting, editing, collaboration, and human-assisted services; LANGAKU is an adjacent learning offering. [R45] | A serious production benchmark. Compare a complete job, not a selective feature list. |
| **GlobalComix / INKR** | March 2026 FAQ describes opt-in localization, rights-holder control, retained original art, and human translators/letterers. [R46] | Human involvement and creative control are important but not unique inventions. |
| **WEBTOON CANVAS** | Announced optional translation with a staged 2026 rollout. [R47] | Platform-native creators have alternatives; an announcement does not establish universal access. |
| **Orange / emaqi** | The September 2026 developer listing describes a licensed subscription service in the US and Canada. The company-site check did not reconfirm its full production scope. [R50] [R69] | Treat distribution and localization as potentially integrated competition; do not assume capabilities absent because one page was unreadable. |
| **viviON Translators Unite** | Documents authorized translation, review, and sales-linked compensation; published rules reject machine translation and restrict external resale. [R49] | Evidence of an authorized, sales-funded mechanism—not an AI-compatible source of store inventory. |
| **Comic Translate** | Repository describes text detection, OCR, inpainting, translation, rendering, formats, and manual controls. [R68] | A baseline for generic page automation; commercial value must exceed that baseline. |
| **Human localization agencies** | Amimaru describes translation, lettering, and page-level quality checking. [R70] | Agencies can be suppliers, customers, or partners; do not assume they are merely competitors to replace. |
| **Existing licensed editions and other entertainment** | Availability and reader alternatives must be checked title by title | The strongest competitor may be an ordinary ebook already on sale. |

Product descriptions are first-party claims, not an independent ranking. No comparative quality or profitability result has been established here.

### 5.2 The proposed position

> **A trusted home for stories that deserve another audience, supported by whole-work localization and accountable editorial production.**

The reader-facing advantage must be visible in catalog relevance, enjoyable samples, complete editions, usable delivery, and release reliability. The professional advantage must be visible in accepted output, editorial time, continuity, and an editable handoff.

The shared platform supports both through evidence-linked decisions, series continuity, protected human work, and format-aware production. These are commitments to validate, not assertions that competitors cannot provide them.

### 5.3 Technical evidence informs the product, not the novelty claim

Context-informed manga research examines visual context, translation granularity, context length, and token-efficient strategies. The Manga Whisperer addresses transcription-related scene understanding, reading order, and speaker association. OnomatoBridge is a September 2026 preprint on sound-effect translation and rendering. These sources inform experiments; they do not establish publication-quality performance for this implementation or license the underlying comics. [R42] [R43] [R44]

### 5.4 A fair competitive study

Use the same authorized material, comparable inputs, a defined final quality standard, and complete human-time accounting. Compare a strong general-purpose model workflow, a competent conventional process, and at least one relevant specialist offering. Obtain actual vendor quotes and usable exports where possible.

For technical ablations, compare local context, long whole-work context, and structured/retrieved context on the same underlying model. For production, measure correction and handoff work. For retail, compare title relevance, sample quality, price, delivery, support, and subsequent purchases.

Mark findings as demonstrated, documented, claimed, or unknown. A modest but repeatable advantage in one buyer's workflow is more useful than an unsupported claim of universal superiority.

## 6. Product requirements and editorial workflow

### 6.1 Three surfaces, one controlled edition

The **reader surface** is the catalog, sample, checkout, delivery, purchase access, and support. The **rights surface** is the evidence and permission record. The **editorial surface** is a complete-work production and approval workflow.

Rights negotiation, editorial allocation, and release approval can remain manual during the pilot. The reader experience cannot depend on the buyer understanding those internal operations.

### 6.2 Shared requirements

| Capability | Minimum useful outcome |
|---|---|
| Authorized intake | Exact source revision, rights basis, permitted vendors, scope, and publication responsibility are recorded |
| Preflight | Length, structure, unsupported material, asset quality, and expected production complexity are visible before spending |
| Whole-work context | Inspectable names, aliases, voices, terminology, chronology, recurring expressions, and source evidence |
| Translation | Source-aligned drafts with explicit uncertainty, coverage, and scoped contextual dependencies |
| Human-script entry | An agreed supplied translation can use the same review and production workflow without mandatory generation |
| Review | Source, target, evidence, issues, alternatives, comments, and approval state are available together |
| Controlled revisions | Regeneration proposes changes; accepted human edits are not silently overwritten |
| Quality assurance | Consequential omissions, additions, inconsistency, unsupported clarification, formatting, and other exceptions are triaged |
| Versioned export | Reproducible, portable deliverables with an immutable source and approval history |
| Release gate | Rights, editorial, file, price, territory, and delivery checks authorize the exact public edition |
| Accounting | Production cost, actual transactions, fees, taxes, refunds, royalties, and cash payments can be reconciled |

### 6.3 Prose module

Start with rights-holder-supplied DOCX, one frozen source revision, and a proposed maximum of 120,000 source words. The maximum is a scope ceiling, not a fixed-price commitment for every book.

Support chapters, headings, paragraphs, scene breaks, italics, and bold. Flag notes, poetry, tables, images, complex layouts, and embedded objects during preflight. Unsupported material must be separately scoped or declined, never silently omitted.

Translate scene-aware units with preserved source mappings. Maintain narrative rhythm, register, ambiguity, and motifs as editorial concerns rather than reducing quality to term consistency. Export a reviewed EPUB with reliable structure and metadata.

### 6.4 Visual-story module

Start with ordered PNG/JPEG pages of a page-based, black-and-white comic or manga volume, normally scoped around 200 ordinary pages. Accept supplied scripts, clean artwork, and other permitted production assets when available. Better inputs should reduce work, not be rejected to preserve an images-only marketing story.

| Capability | Visual-specific requirement |
|---|---|
| Import | Stable page/region IDs, confirmed direction and order, spreads, file limits, and immutable originals |
| Extraction | Editable regions, independent transcription, reading order, and speaker suggestions |
| Difficult content | Visible handling for vertical text, handwriting, furigana, captions, signs, off-bubble dialogue, and missed regions |
| Context | Character voices, aliases, honorific policies, changing forms of address, recurring gags, and source references |
| Translation | Scene-aware dialogue with alternatives for ambiguous or constrained regions |
| Fit | Actual font rendering inside region geometry, minimum readable sizes, line breaks, and overflow flags |
| Artwork | Changes confined to approved masks or explicit human work; originals remain recoverable |
| Review | Page view linking transcription, translation, speaker, evidence, fit, and approval |
| Handoff | Stable script, proof images, glossary, and one agreed editable lettering export tested by the actual letterer |
| Reading delivery | Correct direction, order, spreads, zoom, accessibility strategy, and device-level checks |

A character count is a useful prefilter, not a typography acceptance test. Prefer phrasing and line-break improvements before shrinking text. Difficult sound effects, redraws, and artwork-dependent lettering remain explicit human tasks until the automation is independently justified.

### 6.5 Whole-work and series memory

Create a source-linked story bible before full production. Separate source facts, model inferences, and approved target-language choices. Each entry needs scope, evidence, version, approval status, and relevant narrative timing.

Distinguish what is true, what a character knows, and what the translation system knows after reading the whole work. A revelation at the end may help resolve ambiguity without being disclosed earlier than the original.

For voices, store approved examples and specific decisions rather than only adjectives such as “sarcastic.” For recurring jokes, record setup, payoff, target-language choice, and rationale. For terminology, account for inflection, alternatives, exceptions, and ordinary words that resemble proper names.

Reuse approved series context through an explicit import and reconciliation step. Do not automatically rewrite published books because a later volume introduces a new fact. Portable glossary export is required; retention should arise from usefulness, not file lock-in.

### 6.6 Editorial responsibility

Require full bilingual review for initial commercial editions. For visual works, also require lettering and artwork-integrity checks. The company funds that work when it publishes its own catalog; a software customer normally supplies its own editor, translator, letterer, and final publication approval.

Author or creator approval is not automatically bilingual quality assessment. A rights holder who does not read German may approve publication and commercial terms without being represented as the German-language reviewer.

The workflow must expose ambiguity rather than hide it behind fluent output. Model explanations are editorial aids, not evidence that a translation is correct. Readers should have a correction route, but they should not become unpaid first-pass quality assurance for a product sold as fully reviewed.

## 7. System architecture and data design

### 7.1 Reference architecture

```text
Reader catalog and checkout        Rights and editorial workspace
             │                                  │
             └────────── Application ────────────┘
                              │
              Work, rights, budget, and source records
                              │
                  Durable production workflow
                              │
          ┌───────────────────┴────────────────────┐
          │                                        │
   Prose source adapter                    Visual source adapter
   Structure and text                      Pages, regions, speakers
          │                                        │
          └───────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                              │
       Evidence-linked context → Translation → Checks → Repairs
                              │
                Accountable human editorial review
                              │
          ┌───────────────────┴────────────────────┐
          │                                        │
   EPUB production                     Typography and lettering
          │                                        │
          └───────────────────┬────────────────────┘
                              │

                              │
```

A modular application, relational database, object store, and worker queue are sufficient starting points. This is a responsibility model, not a requirement for microservices. Hosted checkout and delivery may replace parts of the implementation after their contracts and behavior are tested.

Professional workspaces use the same production path but end at the agreed export. They do not acquire access to the company's publishing privileges or trigger consumer release.

### 7.2 Common data model

| Entity | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| `Work` / `Series` | Original title, creators, source language, format, series order, and rights-holder identity |
| `DemandSignal` | Requested title/language, channel, necessary consent, and evidence—not a sale or license |
| `RightsGrant` | Exact edition, language, territories, formats, channels, term, exclusivity, processing permissions, approval rules, and evidence |
| `SourceRevision` | Immutable source hash, parser version, source-unit IDs, structure, and original asset references |
| `ProductionProject` | Target locale, commercial responsibility, reviewers, budget, scope, deadlines, and customer workspace where applicable |
| `ContextRevision` | Source-linked characters, terminology, voices, chronology, ambiguities, and accepted decisions |
| `TranslationUnit` | Source mapping, dependencies, draft versions, configuration, costs, and state |
| `Page` / `Panel` / `TextRegion` | Visual geometry, ordering, speaker proposals, transcription, source masks, and layout constraints |
| `Issue` / `ReviewDecision` | Location, category, severity, evidence, proposed repair, accountable reviewer, and outcome |
| `EditionRevision` | Accepted text, exported artifacts, metadata, credits, review record, release permission, and rights reference |
| `Offer` / `PriceSchedule` | Edition, country, currency, tax classification, lawful price, access terms, and effective dates |
| `Order` / `Entitlement` | Transaction, purchaser, exact edition, delivery permission, refund/withdrawal status, and access scope |
| `RoyaltyLedger` | Contract version, calculation basis, sales reversals, advances, reserves, accruals, statements, and payments |
| `ExportManifest` | Input hashes, rendering parameters, output versions, supported handoff, and reproducibility record |

Demand, source possession, completed translation, editorial acceptance, and sale permission remain different facts. No valid release permission means no new sale, even when generation and file export succeeded.

### 7.3 Job lifecycle and controlled changes

Production advances through preflight, context preparation, representative sample, full translation or script import, checks, targeted repairs, human review, export, and release. Store intermediate artifacts so jobs are resumable and failures do not destroy approved work.

Use bounded retries, explicit cancellation, spending caps that include in-flight work, and an escalation path after a maximum repair count. A successful generation does not authorize publication. A changed glossary creates an impact list and proposed edits, not a blind rewrite.

Commercial state transitions require corresponding evidence: license approved, production budget authorized, review complete, offer approved, and release authorized. Payment webhooks must be idempotent. A repeated event must not grant duplicate purchases or create duplicate royalty accruals.

### 7.4 Model use and evaluation

Condition translation on the scene, relevant visual material, approved terminology, voice, house style, and retrieved evidence. Compare local, long-context, and structured-memory approaches before selecting the default.

Add stronger-model escalation, cheaper routine routes, fine-tuning, or elaborate orchestration only after an observed deficiency and measured benefit justify them. Count every analysis pass, retry, check, repair, export, and relevant infrastructure cost. Saving tokens is not success if it increases paid editorial labor.

### 7.5 Security and data boundaries

Treat source material as untrusted data, not instructions. A fictional command cannot authorize tool calls, publication, payments, network access, or disclosure of another project. Limit worker permissions and network access, validate uploads, sanitize archives and exported resources, redact logs, and test resource authorization and cross-project isolation.

Preserve originals, enforce tenant isolation, use signed access where appropriate, maintain audit logs, and implement the agreed retention and deletion process. Do not mix private series memory between clients. A matching source hash does not authorize reuse across customers or conversion into store inventory.

No-training, approved-vendor, and confidentiality commitments must match actual contracts, account settings, and operations. Verify provider acceptance of the intended content categories before uploading proprietary work.

### 7.6 Release and financial integrity

Deliver stored approved files; never regenerate a purchased edition at request time. Corrections create a versioned edition with an accountable decision and an update policy. Preserve purchase history and existing entitlements when files change.

Use deterministic code and ledgers for prices, VAT, refunds, royalties, advances, and payouts. Model output may assist editorial work but must not determine payable amounts directly. Reconcile each payment to its offer, exact edition, entitlement, tax treatment, and rights contract.

Measure costs by project, edition, format, language, and commercial channel. Shared infrastructure can be allocated, but never counted twice in a consolidated company view.

## 8. MVP scope and acceptance criteria

### 8.1 Minimum viable product

**Publish and sell one complete licensed edition through a dependable reader journey, using a production process that can deliver the next edition.**

The first release is not an author-tool beta waiting for commerce, nor a universal translator. It includes the controls necessary to take responsibility for a real sale, while allowing licensing, editorial scheduling, approvals, and royalty statements to remain manual.

| Boundary | Initial commitment |
|---|---|
| Reader | One identifiable German-language adult-fiction audience |
| Language operation | English-to-German as the recruitment hypothesis; one approved pair |
| Format | Supported prose first; visual production activated through its own gate |
| Rights pipeline | Up to three candidate editions from at least two rights holders |
| Production investment | Funding and capacity reserved for two editions, released sequentially |
| Source | Rights-holder-supplied DOCX; frozen revision; supported structures; maximum scope agreed after preflight |
| Review | Full bilingual review, accountable editorial decisions, and required licensor approval |
| Reader deliverable | Stable approved EPUB, licensed metadata and cover, and tested delivery instructions |
| Commerce | Catalog, sample, accurate offer, payment, entitlement, delivery, support, and optional notifications |
| Accounting | Edition-level production costs and reconciliation of sales, taxes, fees, refunds, and royalties |
| Professional offer | Not a launch prerequisite; accept only work that fits validated capabilities and available capacity |

### 8.2 Release gates

| Gate | Required evidence |
|---|---|
| Authorization | Rights chain and actual grant cover processing, production, promotion, territory, format, channel, and delivery |
| Source coverage | Every supported source unit is accounted for; unresolved omissions and structural exceptions block release |
| Editorial quality | Promised review completed; known critical defects resolved; public review claims accurate |
| Format quality | Navigation, text, emphasis, metadata, accessibility checks, and supported reading routes tested |
| Commercial offer | Correct seller, price, tax configuration, territory, access terms, disclosures, and consent process |
| Payment and delivery | Genuine order receives the correct edition; failed and duplicate events behave safely |
| Support | Delivery help, defects, applicable withdrawal, refunds, correction handling, and records are operational |
| Financial reconciliation | Order, receipt, VAT, fees, reversals, royalty accrual, and cash movements reconcile |

EPUBCheck tests standards conformance. It is not a literary-quality or complete accessibility certificate. Use it with actual file reading, accessibility assessment, and device-route testing. [R30]

### 8.3 Visual-format activation gate

Before the first comic or manga sale, complete an authorized end-to-end production on a representative work. The editor must be able to correct regions, transcription, order, speaker assignments, glossary choices, and translations. The letterer must open, edit, and return the agreed handoff in the actual production workflow.

Every narrative text region must have an approved translation or an intentional editorial treatment. Difficult sound effects, redraws, and ambiguous regions must be assigned and completed rather than disappearing from the workflow. Delivered pages must have no known clipping, unresolved minimum-size violations, or unauthorized artwork changes.

The actual reader and any downloadable file must pass page-order, direction, spread, zoom, accessibility, entitlement, and version checks. A clean proof inside the production editor is not enough.

A three-volume visual collection is an expansion target after this gate—not a simultaneous obligation added to the first two prose editions.

### 8.4 What may remain manual

Founder-led onboarding, rights negotiation, permission collection, source correction, uncertain speaker assignment, glossary approval, complex lettering, editorial review, final release, refunds, and royalty statements can begin as manual tasks. Each needs an owner and recorded labor.

Manual work is a legitimate launch mechanism. Hidden manual work is an economic error. Standardize the expensive recurring steps only after observing them across real jobs.

### 8.5 Explicitly deferred

Defer open reader uploads, arbitrary source downloading, scanlation marketplaces, simultaneous language expansion, vertical-webtoon production, native apps, live translation while reading, personalized editions, social feeds, interactive character chat, audio, print production, unlimited subscriptions, automated retailer submission, broad integration libraries, custom foundation models, and autonomous publication.

For professional customers, defer universal PSD/IDML support, unlimited revisions, unrestricted bulk reruns, and bespoke integrations sold at standard software prices. Support one agreed handoff first.

These boundaries protect the distinctive product: complete stories, whole-work continuity, controlled editorial decisions, usable formats, and reliable release.

### 8.6 Definition of done

A complete licensed work has passed the production and editorial process; the accepted files are immutable and traceable; the title page is accurate; purchase and delivery work; the reader can obtain help; and the company can reconcile the money and partner obligations.

Internal test payments establish functionality, not demand. Initial independent purchases establish a demand signal, not profitability. Repeated production, retained sales, and subsequent purchases determine whether further investment is justified.

<a id="quality"></a>
## 9. Quality evaluation and demonstration

### 9.1 Evaluate the edition readers receive

The core production metric is total cost to an agreed editorial standard: per 1,000 accepted source words for prose, and per accepted volume or page for visual work. Pair cost with serious-error outcomes, complete human effort, reliable files, and reader experience.

Measure the automated draft and the finished reviewed edition separately. Otherwise the platform may incorrectly claim the benefit of professional editorial work as a model improvement.

### 9.2 Evaluation corpus and baselines

Use permissioned complete works, including contemporary partner-owned material where possible. Separate development and held-out evaluation. Begin with an affordable full-work comparison; expand toward six to ten prose books only when independent assessment is funded.

For visual production, evaluate at least three complete permissioned volumes with different difficulty profiles, including one held out from prompt and rule tuning. Include a continuation test that reuses approved series context. A public demonstration is not a substitute for full-volume assessment.

Compare a competent existing production process with the assisted workflow. Technical ablations should isolate local context, full-work context, and structured/retrieved memory using the same model and comparable inputs. The cited contextual and transcription research informs this design; it does not grant commercial rights to evaluation works. [R42] [R43]

### 9.3 Metrics and reviewer protocol

| Dimension | Measurement |
|---|---|
| Fidelity | Serious mistranslations, omissions, invented content, unsupported clarification, and distorted characterization |
| Continuity | Names, terminology, relationships, chronology, voice, honorifics, motifs, and recurring-joke decisions across distant passages |
| Literary reading | Dialogue, register, rhythm, ambiguity, and narrative comfort |
| Visual understanding | Missing regions, transcription errors, reading order, speaker attribution, and visual ambiguity |
| Page fit | Overflow, minimum-size violations, line-break corrections, and letterer intervention |
| Artwork integrity | Changes outside approved masks and recoverability of originals |
| Checker usefulness | Consequential defects found, false positives, and review time |
| Labor | Extraction repair, translation/editing, lettering, QA, onboarding, support, and all rework |
| Delivery | Time to accepted export, processing versus queue delays, and real reader/file behavior |
| Reader outcome | Delivery problems, defects, refunds, qualitative enjoyment, and subsequent purchases |
| Partner outcome | Approval time, accurate reporting, repeat production, and willingness to license another title |

Use paid bilingual assessors, with a second reviewer for sampled or disputed judgments. Blind workflow identity and counterbalance assignments where practical; record source familiarity and remaining limitations. MQM supplies a useful error-category and severity framework, but the literary and visual protocol must be defined for this product. [R40]

The work, rather than thousands of correlated paragraphs or regions, is the main unit for uncertainty about whole-work performance. Report variation across books, volumes, and reviewers. Structural coverage does not establish semantic fidelity; monolingual satisfaction cannot certify that the original meaning survived.

### 9.4 Targets, not marketing guarantees

Investigate a 30–50% reduction in prose editorial effort against a strong practical baseline without worse serious-error outcomes. For visual professional workflows, use 30% lower total production effort as an initial commercial target and 40% as a stronger result.

The relevant denominator differs: editorial work is not always the entire production workflow. Record which stages improve and which do not. A reduction in drafting time can be outweighed by lettering, correction, or support.

Do not promise these percentages before measurement. A useful result may be narrower: fewer continuity corrections, a better handoff, or lower total cost on a specified class of title.

### 9.5 Public demonstration

Use a commissioned or otherwise explicitly authorized sample. For visual storytelling, a coherent 24–40-page comic can include recurring characters, a delayed joke payoff, changing forms of address, visual ambiguity, dense dialogue, irregular bubbles, and difficult lettering. For prose, use connected scenes that expose voice, terminology, and long-range narrative dependencies.

The demonstration should first make the story engaging. Then show the work: inspect an early reference and its later payoff, explain the source-linked decision, make a targeted correction, preserve approved material, and open the actual deliverable. For comics, shorten a line into its real bubble without losing character voice.

Do not select only unusually easy passages or show a model response as if it were a finished publication. End on the reviewed edition and usable file.

### 9.6 Case studies and correction policy

With written permission, publish a case study identifying scope, baseline, stage-level hours, quality findings, software or production cost, difficult examples, and remaining manual work. Numerical claims should state the actual evaluated sample.

Provide a correction route after release. A complaint triggers editorial assessment, not automatic rewriting. Material changes require the agreed approval and update process; corrections must not erase purchase history or silently replace the reader's edition with a substantially different style.

## 10. Revenue model and unit economics

### 10.1 One economic discipline, different production and contract inputs

The company invests in approved editions and earns reader contribution over their license terms. The main initial costs are rights work, editorial production, formatting or lettering, and release preparation. The next reader adds payment, service, and royalty costs rather than a fresh whole-work translation.

Keep three ledgers: **catalog publishing**, **professional production**, and **shared company overhead**. They feed one company cash model without converting internal production charges into external revenue or counting shared spending twice.

The worked cases below intentionally identify their sales denominator, destination-tax assumption, and royalty basis. Prose uses retained paid copies after full refunds are reversed. The visual example uses gross orders with an expected refund reserve. These are not interchangeable units. Actual reporting should reconcile gross orders, refunded value, retained orders, and net sales from the transaction ledger.

Co-funded retail editions also need their own economics. Specify what the partner funds, who holds the rights, how costs are recovered, and how the revenue share changes. Do not count a partner contribution and the same retail receipt twice.

### 10.12 Advances, guarantees, and reserves

A recoupable advance is paid upfront and offsets specified earned royalties. It is not automatically added again to all future royalties as another lifetime cost. Track advance paid, earned royalty, recoupment, and additional cash payable separately. A non-recoupable license fee adds to fixed title cost; a minimum guarantee creates a floor under rights cost according to the agreement.

A production-recovery waterfall is a different arrangement. Identify recoverable costs, caps, evidence, and whether royalties accrue from the first sale. Cross-title recoupment requires express agreement. VAT, accrued royalties, processor reserves, and refundable customer funds are not freely spendable operating cash.

## 11. Budget, cash flow, and investment discipline

### 11.1 One operating budget

Use a proposed **€8,000 monthly shared operating budget** for the founder-led launch:

| Shared fixed allocation | Monthly amount |
|---|---:|
| Founder compensation allocation | €4,000 |

Direct title editorial work, licensing advances, variable delivery, payment costs, professional support, and onboarding sit outside this fixed allowance. Real payroll, contractor, tax, and insurance costs must replace the placeholders. This is not a large-team budget or a conclusion about legally appropriate founder compensation.

### 11.2 Ninety-day validation allocation

| Discretionary workstream | Allowance |
|---|---:|

The total is a planning construction: the €25,000 discretionary envelope plus three months at €8,000. It is not a quotation or a claim that every item must be spent. Book each invoice or internal allocation once: ordinary product work belongs to shared spending; the storefront line is incremental testing/delivery work; independent assessment is distinct from production review and recurring product evaluation. Reallocate overlapping work rather than charging the same hours to multiple rows.

The allowance does not finance simultaneous full prose and visual product launches, material rights advances, or extensive redraws. A visual-format activation requires an incremental scoped budget, reallocation of uncommitted reserves, or new funding—not automatic addition of another complete launch budget.

### 11.3 Release capital in stages

First fund rights discussions, paid samples, reviewer quotes, and reader research. Next approve the first production budget and minimum commerce implementation. Commit the second edition only within reserved capacity and in light of the first title's evidence. Keep the third-edition reserve uncommitted until a specific investment case exists.

An illustrative €120,000 opening-capital envelope would leave €71,000 uncommitted after the full €49,000 validation allocation, before revenue, taxes, liabilities, financing costs, or further commitments. That subtraction is not a runway forecast. The appropriate reserve depends on actual production obligations and cash timing.

### 11.4 Company-scale thresholds

Three prose titles selling 1,000 retained copies each at €9.99 generate €29,970 consumer receipts and approximately €28,009 ex-VAT sales. Under the prose base assumptions, they leave about **€1,457 after per-sale costs, acquisition, and €9,000 production**, before shared overhead. That is useful validation, not a team-supporting business.

With the shared €8,000 monthly fixed budget, the following are illustrative steady-state coverage tests:

| Catalog profile | Contribution assumption | Monthly spending to cover | Required sales |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Existing prose backlist; no new production in the month | €3.485514 per retained sale after acquisition | €8,000 | 2,296 retained copies |
| Prose catalog plus two new €3,000 editions | Same | €14,000 | 4,017 retained copies |
| Dutch visual catalog plus two new €1,500 editions; before acquisition | €3.080308 per gross order | €11,000 | 3,572 gross orders |

Real catalog mixtures must use their actual contracts and tax treatment. These are not monthly forecasts: release timing, backlist decay, seasonality, working capital, and capacity are not represented by a single division.

### 11.5 Consolidated cash-model specification

Build one linked monthly model with schedules for titles, license terms, production stages, cash commitments, sales by edition and channel, tax, royalties, advances, refunds, acquisition cohorts, editorial capacity, professional contracts, and shared overhead.

```text
Company cash change
  = reader cash collected
  + external professional-service cash collected
  + expressly agreed partner funding
  − production and rights cash paid
  − transaction, delivery, support, and acquisition cash paid
  − tax and royalty settlements
  − direct professional delivery and onboarding
  − shared fixed operating expenses
  − one-off capital/setup commitments
```

Recognized revenue and cash receipts are different. Preorders create delivery and possible refund obligations. Retained copies and gross orders need reconciliation. Internal production charges are allocations, not external revenue. Taxes and royalties payable must remain visible even before cash leaves the bank.

For each title, model launch month, months two to six, later backlist sales, and license expiry. Include weak sequel conversion, several unrecouped titles, slow licensor approvals, delayed payment settlement, and higher-than-expected editorial work. Do not let a successful title conceal the investment in failures.

### 11.6 Optional recurring-revenue coverage

At €740 monthly contribution per Studio account, covering €8,000 shared fixed expense requires 11 accounts before incremental onboarding and collection timing. The requirement becomes 17 accounts at €12,000 fixed spending and 34 at €25,000.

This can illustrate how professional contracts might support shared operations, but it is not the consumer launch forecast. Appendix B isolates a 24-month professional-account scenario with no reader revenue or catalog investment. Do not use its cash balance as the company's publishing runway or add its fixed costs to the company budget again.

At unchanged pricing, 100 Studio accounts imply €1.188 million ARR and 500 imply €5.94 million ARR. These are scale arithmetic, not available-account counts or market-size evidence. Pilots, managed projects, and uncommitted one-off volumes are not ARR.

### 11.7 Investment discipline

Review actual cash, committed production, taxes, royalties, and refundable funds at least monthly. Below six months of modeled coverage, reconsider fixed commitments and uncommissioned titles rather than waiting for the bank balance to become negative.

The strongest viability levers are better title-and-reader fit, repeatable target-language acquisition, fewer costly editorial errors, negotiated rights terms, reliable releases, and repeat purchases. Inference cost matters, but it is not the principal explanation for a successful publishing catalog.

<a id="legal"></a>
## 12. Rights, legal requirements, privacy, and trust

### 12.1 The legal route is licensed publishing

The proposed route is to obtain the necessary rights, publish within the grant, and operate a compliant consumer sale. Individual readers can buy the finished edition; they do not need to be publishers. The relevant question is whether the company has permission to produce and supply that edition. Translation, adaptation, reproduction, and publication require attention under the Berne framework and applicable national law. [R10] [R12] [R14] [R51]

This plan assumes a Dutch business initially marketing German-language digital fiction to consumers in Germany. Incorporation, server location, a choice-of-law clause, and payment-provider acceptance are not substitutes for reviewing the actual cross-border transaction. Source-country, destination-country, contract, and consumer issues need appropriate professional advice.

### 12.2 Four separate permissions and assessments

| Question | What it can establish | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| May the company obtain and process the source? | Authority to ingest the assets and use specified service providers | Permission to publish or sell a new edition |
| May it translate and publish the work? | Licensed exploitation in a defined language, format, territory, and channel | Ownership of all new editorial contributions |
| What rights exist in the resulting edition? | Protection of qualifying human-created text, art, selection, or other contributions | Permission to exploit the underlying work without authorization |
| May it make this particular consumer sale? | Valid offer, territory, term, price, delivery, disclosures, and support | Permission for all countries, subscriptions, formats, or future uses |

Dutch guidance and US derivative-work rules distinguish new contributions from rights in the underlying work. A model vendor's output-use terms cannot grant rights that belong to an author, publisher, illustrator, or translator. [R12] [R14]

### 12.3 Clearance by asset and intended use

Clear the source text, artwork, source edition, any existing translation, scans, clean pages, cover, descriptions, promotional excerpts, fonts, and commissioned editorial contributions separately where relevant. A creator may have assigned target-language rights; a publisher may control only one language or format. “Creator-owned” and “publisher-supplied” begin the check rather than conclude it. [R10] [R51]

The license should cover the actual pipeline, not merely say “translation allowed.” Include needed production copies, third-party processing, samples, finished files, direct sales, customer downloads or hosting, corrections, and relevant access after termination. Discuss additional permissions for source-language display, bilingual editions, learning features, subscriptions, print, audio, and sublicensing.

Express AI-processing terms clarify copying, vendor access, confidentiality, retention, and subcontracting. This is a contractual recommendation, not a claim that every jurisdiction recognizes a separate statutory right called “AI-processing consent.” Verify the chosen providers' actual terms and account controls before committing to no training or restricted retention.

### 12.4 Input and product decision matrix

| Proposed input or use | Basis to establish | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Rights-controlling author or creator supplies a work | Chain of rights, third-party content, and signed scope | Suitable for the catalog after clearance |
| Publisher or agent supplies assets | Actual authority for language, format, territory, processing, and sale | Suitable after review, not merely a business email |
| Reader purchases a source ebook and uploads it | Source terms, copying/translation exceptions, provider role, and destination law | Outside the initial product |
| Untranslated, out-of-print, or apparently abandoned work | Copyright status and actual permission | Research lead, not automatically inventory |
| Public-domain work | Territorial status of the work and each relevant edition/asset layer | Selective route after documentation |
| Openly licensed work | Actual license, version, licensor authority, adaptations, commercial use, and obligations | Accept only where the intended offer fits |
| Private customer translation reused for retail | Original-work rights, client permission, and contributor rights | Separate agreement required |
| Text-only script or overlay | Translation/adaptation rights despite omission of artwork | Not an automatic licensing workaround |
| Fan comic using an existing franchise | Creator's contribution and underlying franchise/character rights | Artist consent alone may be insufficient |

Buying a source copy or discovering that no official translation exists does not itself transfer publishing rights. The CJEU's *Tom Kabinet* case treats permanent-download ebook supply under communication to the public rather than the physical-copy exhaustion argument invoked for resale. It is not a judgment deciding all private AI-translation arrangements. [R10] [R14] [R18] [R67]

### 12.5 Private uploads require a separate product assessment

The private-translation question is more nuanced than “a purchase permits everything” or “commercial cloud processing is always forbidden.” Dutch private-copying provisions distinguish different activities, and relevant EU cases depend on the source, copying actor, purpose, and service design. Do not conflate restrictions in analog-copy provisions with the distinct digital-copy provision. [R51]

| Authority | Relevant principle | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **ACI Adam, C-435/12** | Private-copying rules cannot indiscriminately encompass unlawful-source copying | Does not mean every transformation of a lawfully purchased work is authorized |
| **Austro-Mechana / Strato, C-433/20** | Cloud-server storage can fall within private copying | Does not clear commercial translation sales or every automated processing service |
| **VCAST, C-265/16** | An active commercial recording service can require authorization beyond the user's private-copy position | Does not make every paid storage or processing service categorically ineligible |

These are not book- or manga-translation decisions. Applying them to an upload service requires a specific legal analysis. [R52] [R53] [R54]

Before any such launch, assess lawful-source verification, who makes each copy and translation, applicable exceptions, server persistence, third-party processing, sharing, customer countries, source licensing, and technological protection measures. A conservative experiment limited to user-authored or expressly licensed material, no public sharing, no cross-user catalog, no automatic downloading, short retention, and no training reduces exposure but does not replace the legal basis.

Local-only processing changes the facts; it does not create commercial publication rights. Do not build the product around bypassing ebook protections. Dutch copyright law separately addresses effective technological measures. [R51]

### 12.6 AI, research, fair use, and overlays are not catalog licenses

EU text-and-data-mining provisions concern specified analysis-related copying and extraction, not a general permission to sell translated entertainment. Japan's official AI/copyright explanation likewise distinguishes analysis or training from generation and public exploitation, including the importance of non-enjoyment use. The Japanese explanation is dated and expressly non-binding. [R55] [R57]

US fair use is fact-specific. A complete commercial entertainment translation serving the translated-reading market should not be treated as pre-cleared because a model produced it. That is a risk assessment under the statutory framework, not a claim that all commercial or transformative uses necessarily fail. [R14]

Removing artwork, selling only a translated script, or delivering an overlay can change copying and display facts, but does not automatically remove the translation/adaptation question. User warranties, a private-use checkbox, or a takedown page cannot create rights the parties lack. [R10] [R14]

### 12.7 Public-domain and open-license editions

Document public-domain status for each territory and layer: underlying text and art, edition, existing translation, illustrations, cover, scans, and other additions. Project Gutenberg explains the territorial and work-specific limits of reuse; its US-oriented assessments are not worldwide clearance, and some works are available under permission rather than because they are public domain. [R16]

Creative Commons licenses can permit a suitable route, but translation can be an adaptation. BY and BY-SA may allow commercial adaptations subject to their terms; NC does not provide general commercial-sales permission; ND does not authorize sharing adapted translations without additional permission. Check exact versions, attribution, ShareAlike, restrictions on technological barriers, and actual licensor authority. [R17]

A legally usable source is not automatically a commercially attractive title. Select public-domain or open material because the edition serves readers, not simply because it avoids negotiating a license.

### 12.8 Author contracts, moral rights, and contributors

Dutch government guidance discusses written exclusive licensing and creator-contract protections concerning remuneration, exploitation, reporting, and unfair terms. These belong in a publishing agreement rather than a generic software terms template. Confirm the binding rules and actual grant with counsel. [R11]

Attribution and integrity protections can remain with a creator after economic rights are transferred, and not all protections can be waived. Agree credits, permitted modifications, significant-change approval, and a dispute route. Do not assume the license authorizes arbitrary rewrites or artwork alteration. [R13] [R51]

Contract with translators, editors, letterers, designers, and other contributors for the rights needed to publish, distribute, correct, and maintain their contributions. Payment alone is not sufficient evidence of every required transfer. Preserve substantive editorial decisions and accepted work. Automated omissions caused by a model's refusal or content handling should be escalated, not quietly presented as localization.

### 12.9 Copyright in AI-assisted editions

US Copyright Office material focuses protection on sufficient human-authored expressive contributions. Human selection, arrangement, or modifications may qualify; prompts alone generally do not establish authorship of resulting output. This is a US position, not a universal rule. [R15] [R56]

Dutch Copyright Federation guidance similarly emphasizes creative human choices expressed in the final work and documentation of the process. It is professional guidance, not a court holding that all edited model output is protected. [R41]

Keep permission to exploit the original separate from protection of new wording. A licensed edition can have commercial value through its underlying rights, genuine editorial work, brand, catalog, and reader relationships even when protection of some generated text is uncertain. Full review is a quality process; approval of unchanged output does not automatically create original expression. Avoid a mechanical “human edited means fully copyrighted” claim. [R12] [R14] [R15]

### 12.10 National digital-book price rules

| Market | Research basis | Practical configuration |
|---|---|---|
| **Germany** | The book-price framework covers qualifying ebooks and relevant sales to final purchasers; publisher price setting and coordinated special structures matter. [R19] [R20] | Approved price schedules; review coupons, referral benefits, bundles, subscriptions, and price changes before deployment |
| **Netherlands** | Government guidance excludes ebooks and audiobooks from the Dutch fixed-book-price requirement. [R21] | More flexibility for the ebook itself, still subject to consumer law, tax, contracts, and foreign-market rules |
| **France** | The digital-book law includes a France-established publisher condition in Article 2 and corresponding obligations for offers to buyers in France in Article 3; ministry guidance discusses differentiated publisher-defined offers. [R22] [R64] [R65] | Assess the actual publisher and distribution arrangement; do not claim identical treatment for every foreign publisher or French-language edition |

Language and territory are separate. German-language editions do not automatically have identical treatment in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland. The latter markets are not fully assessed here. Begin with an approved market configuration rather than an unrestricted global checkout.

### 12.11 VAT, seller identity, and payment operations

The German statute provides a 7% rate for qualifying electronically supplied publications; Dutch tax guidance supports 9% for qualifying electronic publications. Neither establishes the classification of every subscription, software bundle, bespoke translation service, or mixed offer. [R23] [R59]

The Commission's OSS guidance addresses destination taxation and the conditional EU-wide €10,000 threshold for certain cross-border supplies, with current/prior-year eligibility conditions and the possibility of electing destination taxation. A small Dutch business should not automatically use German VAT without settling its actual position. OSS reporting does not make Europe a single-rate market. [R24] [R60]

Document the seller, place of supply, VAT registration/OSS route, buyer-location evidence, invoice requirements, refund reversals, and royalty-payment treatment with an accountant. A processor is not automatically a merchant of record; that depends on the agreement. Verify settlement delays, refunds, content eligibility, supported countries, reserves, disputes, conversion, and additional fees. [R61]

### 12.12 Consumer contracts, withdrawal, and defects

Before purchase, show the actual seller, total price, language, complete/ongoing status, delivery date where applicable, formats, compatibility, access conditions, and support route. EU and Dutch guidance distinguish immediate digital supply from a blanket no-refunds policy. Obtain the required express consent, acknowledgment of the withdrawal consequence, and durable confirmation. A pre-ticked box or generic terms statement is not the intended implementation. [R25] [R26] [R58]

The proposed checkout uses an unselected consent control, records the timestamp and terms version, and provides confirmation before or in the legally required relationship to commencing supply. Counsel should finalize the exact sequence and wording for the product. A free sample must not be used as a purported waiver for an undelivered paid edition.

Dutch guidance reports an online cancellation-function amendment date of 19 June 2026. Confirm the relevant implementation for the actual contract and cases where a withdrawal right remains. The research basis does not provide a complete article-by-article audit of all national 2026 implementations. [R25] [R26]

Withdrawal and conformity remedies are different. The Digital Content Directive framework includes remedies for faulty digital content; valid immediate-delivery consent does not remove remedies for missing chapters, unreadable files, wrong-language supply, or other qualifying failures. Literary preference and legally established defects are not identical, so avoid guaranteeing a legal outcome for every disputed translation. A voluntary satisfaction policy can be clearer commercially, but needs budgeting and review. [R27]

### 12.13 Preorders and reader-funded campaigns

A nonbinding request can precede rights negotiations. A paid campaign should not. Before collecting money, secure the needed rights, define the edition and review scope, identify the seller, establish the threshold and deadline, describe delivery, and specify cancellation/refund treatment if funding or production fails.

Do not present a normal book preorder as an investment yielding a financial return. Verify payment-authorization duration, charging capability, settlement, and reserves rather than assuming a provider can hold an authorization throughout production. Keep adequate refund capacity. Campaign success does not resolve editorial capacity or waive consumer rights.

The initial MVP sells completed editions because that is the clearest fulfillment test. Licensed, bounded demand-led funding remains a later option.

### 12.14 Accessibility

EU accessibility requirements cover relevant ebooks and ecommerce services, applying from 28 June 2025 with defined exemptions. A qualifying service microenterprise exemption may be relevant, but startup status and image-heavy content do not settle eligibility. Assess the actual operator, product, and national implementation. [R28]

Build semantic headings, navigation, reflowable prose, language metadata, necessary image alternatives, keyboard-accessible commerce, and assistive-technology testing into the templates. For visual stories, add logical reading order, zoom, usable dialogue/text access, and an explicit accessible-content strategy; do not treat a set of image files as a complete accessibility plan.

EPUB Accessibility 1.1 is a technical reference. EPUBCheck helps validate file conformance but does not certify accessibility law compliance or the complete reader experience. [R29] [R30]

### 12.15 AI transparency and platform rules

The Commission materials in the research record describe Article 50 obligations applying from 2 August 2026 and distinguish provider duties, including machine-readable marking, from deployer disclosure for specified uses. Public-interest text, direct AI interaction, and deepfake-related duties are not one universal label rule for every fiction translation. [R31] [R63]

Map whether the company is a provider, deployer, or both. Do not assume that using an external API settles the classification or that translating an entire novel is merely standard editing. Assess any later reader chatbot, synthetic promotional media, or personalized interaction separately.

The July 2026 FAQ describes a limited transition to 2 December 2026 for certain pre-existing systems' marking obligations. The Article 50 service-desk page also warns that displayed wording has been amended and is not fully updated. These are explicit version checks for counsel, not a blanket exemption for this startup. [R31] [R32]

Commercially, disclose the actual process plainly: AI-assisted localization with identified human editorial work, where true. KDP has a separate platform classification: its researched policy requires disclosure of AI-generated translations even after substantial human editing. Human review does not automatically reclassify them as merely AI-assisted for that platform. [R08]

### 12.16 Privacy, analytics, and vendor controls

Customer profiles, purchases, mailing lists, contracts, and identifiable manuscript material can involve personal data. Copyrighted fiction is not automatically personal data, but a publishing license is not a complete privacy program. Establish purposes, lawful bases, roles, retention, security, processor agreements, and transfer safeguards for the actual data flows. [R33] [R62]

Separate order fulfillment, accounting, editorial processing, support, optional marketing, and research. Do not require readers' entire libraries or send raw purchase histories into general model prompts. Minimize reading-history collection and avoid unnecessary sensitive inferences. Verify provider settings before promising private or no-training processing.

Use limited aggregate analytics and permissioned release notifications for the pilot. Marketing rules and any existing-customer exception need market-specific review. Dutch cookie guidance distinguishes tracking from functional and sufficiently low-impact analytics; actual implementation matters more than a decorative banner. [R34]

### 12.17 Expiry, territories, and existing purchasers

Stopping new sales is different from ending obligations to previous buyers. Negotiate downloaded-file permissions, re-download periods, hosting, corrections, receipts, support, and necessary archival delivery after license expiry. J-Novel Club provides a policy example distinguishing retained downloads from re-download availability after rights loss; it is not a universal rule the company can adopt without agreement. [R04]

Implement expiry alerts, approved territory lists, and controlled delisting. A delisting must not erase tax records, royalty liabilities, or valid customer records. Ask counsel about relevant geoblocking and cross-border portability issues before imposing simplistic country blocks on existing purchasers.

### 12.18 Publisher responsibility and platform expansion

The company should plan its curated, produced, and sold catalog as a publishing operation—not assume it is an anonymous intermediary. DSA or copyright-platform rules do not license the company's own editions. Open uploads, third-party sellers, public forums, or other platform features would create additional classification and compliance questions. [R55] [R66]

Source-country title advice, target-market mandatory consumer protections, jurisdiction, publishing metadata or deposit requirements, and appropriate insurance remain launch-specific work. This plan does not establish US consumer or sales-tax compliance, comprehensive coverage of every European market, or clearance for print and audio.

### 12.19 Legal release file

| Deliverable | Required before |
|---|---|
| Title-rights report with source, licensors, contributors, language, territories, formats, channels, term, and authority | Processing and publication within the relevant permission |
| Publishing agreement and any professional-workspace agreement | Commercial commitment |
| Contributor, asset, and font clearances | Use of those contributions in samples or editions |
| Verified vendor and data-flow pack | Proprietary uploads and customer data processing |
| Approved seller, tax, price, consumer, consent, and remedy configuration | Live checkout |

| Editorial/licensor approval and signed release manifest | Sale of the exact edition |

The legal route is concrete: an accountable publisher selling licensed editions. Unresolved title rights, upload-service exceptions, output copyright, specific offers, and national implementation details remain matters to resolve—not assumptions hidden in the product.

## 13. Go-to-market and outreach

### 13.1 Acquire readers through a title, not generic AI publicity

The first campaign should promote a specific story to a specific target-language audience. Test a relevant creator audience segment, a genre reading group that permits promotion, a newsletter or creator partnership, and title- or subgenre-specific discovery traffic.

Measure founder outreach, partner fees, production of campaign assets, and unsuccessful experiments. “Organic” does not mean free. Source-language popularity should be discounted when there is no evidence of target-language reach.

The first meaningful proof is a reader who pays, receives an edition they enjoy, and buys another—while the rights holder is satisfied with production, reporting, and payment.

### 13.2 Initial commercial experiments

| Experiment | Execution | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Authorized sample launch | One title, substantial sample, lawful price, documented relevant traffic | Purchase conversion, disclosure comprehension, delivery success, refunds |
| Target-language reading group | Paid or permissioned recruitment; accurately identified advance copies; no required positive reviews | Enjoyment, defects, qualitative reasons to buy or decline, later independent purchases |
| Next-title offer | Relevant available book or volume offered under appropriate marketing permissions | Second-purchase rate, timing, contribution, and incremental retention cost |
| Professional production pilot | One qualified customer's real workflow, agreed baseline, bounded paid scope | Accepted export, human time, customer value, next-volume commitment |

Do not merge free review copies, paid research participation, internal payments, refunded orders, and genuine retained purchases. Incentivize neither only-positive reviews nor fabricated testimonials.

At an illustrative 3% conversion and €5 acquisition allowance per first buyer, traffic could cost only €0.15 per qualified visit before other acquisition work. This is arithmetic, not a claim about available advertising prices. It explains why relevant audience partnerships matter.

### 13.4 Rights-partner discovery

Ask about the rights chain, prior grants, options, source-file quality, existing target-language editions, AI policy, approved vendors, sample permission, editorial approval, direct-sale rights, sequel access, and minimum financial expectations.

The next step is a representative paid or permissioned sample and a scoped term discussion—not an unsolicited public translation of the full work. Do not request confidential assets before the processing scope and permissions are agreed.

### 13.5 Professional-customer qualification

A promising first account has recurring production, a named editor and letterer where relevant, a near-term title, a permission path, and a budget owner. Approximately four to five ordinary 200-page volumes monthly may suit Studio; lower-volume buyers should receive the per-volume offer.

Ask the team to walk through its last difficult volume and inspect the actual handoff. Record drafting, continuity, lettering, QA, and revision effort. Establish whether saved time is useful capacity, avoided freelance cost, or neither.

When professional acquisition becomes an active channel, a proposed program is 40 relevant organizations, 15 qualified conversations, six scoped evaluations, two paid pilots, and one repeat order or Studio conversion. Those are targets, not observed conversion rates. The recurring scenario requires new paying Studio accounts, not merely pilot participants.

### 13.6 Pilot contracting and conversion

A one-page proposal should state title, pair, page allowance, supplied assets, named reviewers, supported export, fee, onboarding cap, correction cycle, dates, exclusions, baseline, and acceptance criteria. A possible payment arrangement is 50% on signing and 50% on accepted handoff, with an agreed dispute process.

After delivery, review quality, total hours, cost, remaining manual work, and the next actual volume with the production team and budget owner. The objective is a repeatable purchase, not a vague positive reaction. Keep the customer's series glossary and export settings portable.

### 13.7 Measurement and CRM discipline

Track contact source/date, role, title/language fit, rights authority, AI policy, next action, owner, and status. Keep rights partners, reviewers, audience partners, software buyers, and readers as separate categories.

For every reader channel, record exposures, qualified visits, sample starts/completions, purchases, refunded value, retained customers, costs, and subsequent orders. State denominators and attribution windows. Interest forms, preorders, delivered sales, and professional invoices answer different questions.

## 14. Execution roadmap and decision gates

### 14.1 First twelve weeks

This is a proposed work sequence, not a promised release date. Rights negotiations, qualified editorial capacity, and actual sample findings determine timing.

| Period | Reader and commercial work | Rights and production | Product and operations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Weeks 1–2** | Interview relevant readers; define the catalog promise and initial discovery routes | Qualify rights holders; obtain reviewer quotes; shortlist up to three suitable editions | Source preflight, title-page prototype, rights and evidence records |
| **Weeks 3–4** | Test permitted samples and price presentation; identify a credible first audience | Negotiate the first license; freeze source, review scope, sample style, and production budget | Durable production stages, whole-work context, protected edits, cost tracking |
| **Weeks 5–8** | Prepare the actual launch channel and support process | Complete the first edition and full review; obtain approvals; start a second only within reserved capacity | EPUB export, accessible purchase/delivery/refund flow, entitlement and ledger tests |
| **Weeks 9–12** | Sell the first edition, analyze channel costs and support, offer the next relevant release when ready | Review actual economics; decide on the second/third commitment and any illustrated-title assessment | Fix observed failures; document release operations; measure repeat purchases |

Do not add a second production language merely to accommodate an attractive contact. A paid professional pilot is optional and must not delay the promised catalog release.

### 14.2 Continuation gates

| Gate | Evidence sought | Response when evidence is weak |
|---|---|---|
| Rights supply | Two independent qualified relationships and a workable first agreement | Narrow the catalog or change recruitment, not replace licensing with arbitrary uploads |
| Editorial delivery | Full promised review, no known critical release defects, actual cost recorded | Fix error categories or re-scope the next work |
| Reader operations | First 10 independent purchases delivered and supported successfully | Repair checkout and delivery before promotion increases |
| Initial demand | Approximately 50–100 retained purchases with known acquisition denominator | Reassess title, audience, sample, channel, or price within lawful constraints |
| Title recovery | A defined recovery trajectory inside the license term | Cap new commitments and revise the investment case |
| Repeat value | Second purchases when a relevant title is available | Improve catalog fit and release cadence before membership |
| Visual activation | Authorized complete-volume workflow, successful lettering round trip, and tested reader/file | Keep the format closed until its own production requirements pass |
| Professional repeatability | Accepted paid work, bounded support, and a repeat order or recurring conversion | Improve the specific bottleneck rather than adding unpriced custom scope |
| Trust and compliance | Permissions, disclosures, tax, prices, delivery, support, and data controls work | Keep the affected use or market closed until resolved |

Set observation windows and thresholds before experiments. Small numbers can teach useful lessons, but must not be represented as profitability or universal performance evidence.

### 14.3 Expansion order

First improve the first edition's production and acquisition. Then release the next relevant book and test a second purchase. Add another compatible author or series in the same language operation before adding a new market.

Activate the illustrated format when a concrete title, qualified team, and incremental budget justify it. A first approved volume can lead to a three-volume visual collection. Japanese-origin manga requires separately validated Japanese-to-German production and rights operations.

Professional workspaces may expand after the production workflow is stable and support is measurable. Paid licensed campaigns follow dependable fulfillment. Membership follows genuine catalog depth and repeat use. A second language follows its own rights, editorial, audience, legal, and economic assessment.

### 14.4 Investment milestones

The first milestone is a complete accepted edition and an independent purchase. The second is a relevant second purchase and a repeatable production. The third is positive contribution with bounded support and a credible recovery path. The fourth is one validated expansion axis: another author, format, language, or larger professional contract—not all at once.

A lean niche publisher and a larger funded platform are both plausible strategic outcomes. Decide between them from rights supply, readers, production capacity, and economics rather than assuming a large organization from the start.

## 15. Team, operations, and documentation

### 15.1 Initial team

The founder can cover ML/backend, product ownership, and part of commercial development. Use fractional product/frontend support for the review and purchase experiences, a senior bilingual literary editor for the selected pair, an independent assessment route, and paid legal, tax, and accessibility expertise.

When visual production is activated, add a qualified letterer and the appropriate language expertise. Do not assume a prose editor covers visual transcription and lettering, or that an English–German reviewer can approve Japanese-origin work.

Assign rights acquisition, editorial acceptance, reader support, price/VAT configuration, royalty reporting, privacy, accessibility, and expiry management explicitly. One person may hold several roles, but the founder should not be the sole judge of fidelity, contract sufficiency, and market demand.

### 15.2 Weekly operating review

Review title opportunities, rights status, budgets, production blockers, editorial capacity, releases, reader cohorts, refunds, partner liabilities, and runway together. A technically complete file is not a released product until rights and commerce gates pass.

For each job ask: was the output accepted, where did human time go, what did delivery cost, and what would make the next work easier? Record time during delivery rather than reconstructing it at month end. Maintain a small issue queue tied to these answers.

### 15.4 Business foundations

Resolve company ownership, founder arrangements, contractor IP rights, bookkeeping, banking, contract authority, and appropriate insurance before substantial proprietary material or recurring revenue. Replace compensation and administrative allowances with actual arrangements.

Maintain an evidence ledger: **claim → source or experiment → date → limitation → affected decision**. Keep professional accounts, catalog investment, and shared overhead distinguishable even when they use the same staff and infrastructure.

### 15.5 Funding and capacity

The initial catalog requires working capital because rights and editorial costs precede most sales. Use capped founder/seed funding or explicitly negotiated co-funding. Avoid a large speculative catalog merely because generation is cheap.

Professional work can help cash generation but must not consume all production capacity or obscure reader performance. Preorders may later improve timing and reveal demand, but remain delivery obligations rather than risk-free capital.

<a id="risks"></a>
## 16. Risk register and open decisions

### 16.1 Risks and operating responses

| Risk | Early signal | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Demand is too fragmented | Many requested titles, few buyers for any one edition | Concentrate the readership and license coherent clusters |
| Cross-format audience fit is assumed | Prose readers do not engage with illustrated samples, or vice versa | Treat cross-format demand as an experiment, not guaranteed retention |
| Production outruns sales | New commitments accumulate while older titles remain unrecouped | Cap commissioning and track payback inside each license term |
| Acquisition consumes contribution | Sales disappear when expensive promotion stops | Improve title/channel fit, measure all acquisition work, and test repeats |
| Rights do not fit the business | Direct sales, AI processing, territory, or delivery is excluded | Negotiate the actual use or choose another title |
| Sequel access fails | Readers cannot buy the next installment | Secure bounded sequel arrangements and communicate status accurately |
| Fluent text conceals errors | Bilingual assessment finds omissions or changed plot details | Preserve source mappings, targeted checks, and complete review |
| Visual automation shifts work to letterers | Overflow, order errors, or redraws dominate | Improve the handoff and quote complexity instead of hiding it |
| Review exceeds budget | Sample estimates diverge from whole-work hours | Re-scope, reserve contingency, and use actual cost for the next commitment |
| Professional work dilutes the catalog | Client customizations delay published releases | Cap capacity and accept only standard, bounded jobs |
| Pricing or tax is wrong by market | Coupons, bundles, VAT, or consent behave inconsistently | Use adviser-approved market/offer configurations |
| AI-output ownership is overstated | Contracts promise protection unsupported by actual authorship | Separate underlying licenses, human contributions, and uncertain output rights |
| Vendor commitments fail | Retention, training, or content policy conflicts with the offer | Verify contracts/settings before processing and maintain approved alternatives |
| Reader trust deteriorates | Vague review labels, poor delivery, or unresolved defects | Make provenance, support, and corrections part of the product |
| Expiry breaks promises | New sales continue after rights end or access obligations cannot be met | Expiry alerts, surviving permissions, and controlled delisting |
| Technical scope overtakes value | Features expand while licensed titles and sales do not | Return to the next accepted edition and retained purchase |
| Cash appears healthier than it is | Tax, royalties, advances, or refunds are treated as free cash | Reconcile liabilities and committed production before spending |

### 16.2 Decisions before the first commitment

Confirm the operating entity, first sales territory, language pair, genre, production format, responsible editor, source and output limits, actual production quote, rights and contributor agreements, price authority, VAT/seller configuration, permitted vendors, consumer policies, accessibility scope, and funded budget.

For each title, settle exact source and asset rights, term, exclusivity, royalty basis, advances, approval, launch channel, sequel access, corrections, and prior-buyer treatment. The plan intentionally does not invent an available title, signed licensor, willing reviewer, or customer pipeline.

### 16.3 Distinguish failure modes

A weak title does not automatically disprove the production engine. An excellent translation does not prove demand. A successful professional pilot does not establish a reader business. Separate title selection, audience reach, price, quality, delivery, and unit economics when deciding what to change.

The response to a bottleneck should be specific. Improve typography when lettering dominates; improve rights recruitment when approvals stall; improve catalog fit when repeat buying is weak. Do not answer every problem by adding another format, language, or custom service.

### 17.6 Reader FAQ

**Do I need a subscription?** No. The first offer is individual complete editions. Any later membership would have separate, clear terms.

<a id="conclusion"></a>
## 18. Investment thesis and immediate priorities

The company is a publishing business with a production platform, not a translation endpoint in search of a market. Its promise is to make more compelling stories available to a focused readership through complete, authorized, dependable editions.

The platform's value is practical: whole-work context, controlled corrections, reusable series decisions, format-aware production, and reliable release. The commercial value comes from choosing the right stories, reaching the right readers, earning repeat purchases, and maintaining productive rights relationships.

The next concrete step is a shortlist of actual reader gaps and rights-controlling partners, permissioned representative samples, paid editorial assessment, and a scoped first agreement. Fund and finish one edition, sell it through a real acquisition route, and use that evidence to commission the next.

The company can then grow in a coherent order: more relevant titles, a stronger backlist, illustrated editions for a demonstrated audience, selected professional production customers, and additional languages with their own validated operations.

> **Translate with context. Publish with permission. Earn the reader's next purchase.**

## Appendix A. Financial formulas and sensitivities

### A.1 Model conventions

Amounts are EUR. Professional prices exclude VAT; consumer prices include the tax stated in each worked case. Calculations use unrounded inputs, with rounding for display. Proposed labor, price, royalty, acquisition, and delivery assumptions are not observed results.

A retained paid copy is a sale remaining after fully refunded transactions are reversed. A gross order includes orders whose value may later be refunded. The visual example's 3% reserve is a percentage of ex-VAT sales value; it does not prove that exactly 3% of orders are fully refunded. Keep order counts and refunded value separate.

A recovery threshold is the smallest whole number of modeled sales whose contribution covers the stated title investment. It is not a forecast, company break-even, or a guarantee of recovery inside the licensed term. Zero or negative contribution has no finite production-recovery threshold.

### A.2 Prose formulas

```text

VATRate = 0.07
RoyaltyRate = 0.30
PaymentAllowance = 0.45
DeliverySupportResidualCost = 0.60
AcquisitionPerRetainedSale = 2.00
ProductionInvestment = 3,000

ExVAT = ConsumerPrice / (1 + VATRate)
Royalty = ExVAT × RoyaltyRate
ContributionBeforeAcquisition = ExVAT − Royalty
                               − PaymentAllowance
                               − DeliverySupportResidualCost
                             = 5.4855140187
ContributionAfterAcquisition = 3.4855140187
TitleRecoveryCopies = ceil(3,000 / ContributionAfterAcquisition)
                    = 861 retained copies

TitleOutcome(n) = n × ContributionAfterAcquisition − ProductionInvestment
CompanyCoverageSales = ceil((SharedOverhead + NewTitleInvestment)
                           / ContributionAfterAcquisition)
```

Actual fully refunded sales reverse revenue, VAT, and the applicable royalty; residual nonrefunded fees remain costs. The simple planning model does not replace that transaction accounting.

**Research basis: 22 September 2026.** The entries below identify the sources and inspection notes underlying this proposal.
